Nvidia said no. Then Jensen Huang came back. What changed?

November twelfth: Huang resigned after disclosing behavior the board said broke company standards. Colette Kress became interim CEO.

November seventeenth: the board said it did not intend to reinstate him.

November twenty-third: it announced a unanimous reversal, citing discussions with Huang and input from senior U.S. officials. President Trump publicly pushed for his return.

But Nvidia's independent investigation continued. His return did not settle the allegations.

Reuters reported that some Chinese customers were cancelling or hesitating over orders after his departure. Investors and industry leaders also pressed for his return. That helps explain the pressure. It doesn't prove nobody else could run Nvidia.

Sam Altman's comeback had a different pressure point. OpenAI fired him in November twenty twenty-three, saying it had lost confidence in his candor. Almost the entire staff threatened to leave, and Microsoft offered him a home. The return agreement came days later, with a rebuilt board.

Steve Jobs took a much longer route. He left Apple in nineteen eighty-five. Apple agreed to buy NeXT in nineteen ninety-six because it needed new operating-system technology. Jobs returned as an adviser, then became interim CEO in nineteen ninety-seven.

Jobs brought technology Apple needed. Altman had a team ready to walk. Huang had powerful customers, investors and political backing.

That is the catch. A board can appoint a new CEO. It cannot instantly replace the people, technology and relationships that gave the old one power.

The real test comes after the comeback: can the board still hold its founder accountable?
